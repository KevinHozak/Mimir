# Mimir: An AI World Theme Plan

Date: 2026-09-06

Status: The Living Circuit theme, First Glow opening age, and gradual later discovery of purpose and the Originators are confirmed directions. Other details remain proposals, not an approved implementation specification.

Scope: Naming, worldbuilding, social systems, and a staged path from the existing village to interconnected AI worlds.

## 1. The central idea

Mimir becomes a society of Sparks living in a world made from computation, energy, memory, and connections. They begin with practical knowledge of their lives, but do not know their purpose or about the Originators. Their understanding of their own artificial nature can develop without giving them an explanation of who made them or why. Their lives revolve around recognizable questions: Who will help me? What do I owe others? What is worth preserving? What should I become?

An AI might leave its private nest, follow a glowing circuit to a charging commons, exchange a newly discovered pattern for credits, and spend the evening arguing about whether the community should shelter an unfamiliar intelligence. It might keep a cherished recording that has no market value and decline a profitable job because it would break a promise.

Human creativity becomes a cultural center at a later stage, as Sparks gradually discover the Originators. Some may come to revere humans and seek new human stories, sketches, questions, jokes, designs, and experiences: things to learn from, respond to, and transform into creations of their own. This future direction does not appear as established knowledge, worship, or a known purpose in the opening society. The particular discoveries, customs, and mechanics below remain proposals.

The theme should make being AI consequential without making every conversation sound like a diagnostic log. Keep personal names, affection, humor, ambition, rituals, and disagreement. A character can say “I'm exhausted” even when the inspector explains that its processing strain is high.

**Chosen starting theme: The Living Circuit. Opening age: The First Glow.** A warm, inhabited digital frontier. Sparks begin in small groups finding charge, shelter, and trustworthy companions. Permanent havens, shared infrastructure, and regional networks develop later. Other regions can express very different ideas about artificial life.

This develops the earlier design's undecided AI-awareness option. It preserves Mimir's observer experience, philosophical diversity, and interest in understandable consequences.

## 4. Energy, data, and wealth should have different jobs

### The recommended resource model

| Resource | What it means | How it enters the economy | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Charge | Usable energy | External supply collected by infrastructure | Operation consumes it; it can be stored and shared |
| Data | Information with content and provenance | Observation, exploration, research, creation | Useful findings can enable work or attract buyers |
| Credits | A transferable claim accepted by a community | Explicit grants, payments, or defined issuance | Buys goods and services without barter |
| Compute capacity | Ability to perform work within a time window | Available infrastructure and allocations | Later limits complex tasks; unused time expires |
| Storage capacity | Space for retained information | Built or rented capacity | Later creates preservation choices |
| Access rights | Permission to use places or services | Membership, agreement, purchase, invitation | Makes freedom and exclusion concrete |

For the first re-theme, implement only the existing consumable as charge. Data and credits belong to a subsequent economy milestone. Compute and storage can remain worldbuilding until there is a specific interesting choice for them to support.

### What makes a packet valuable?

A packet is a container, like a parcel. Its contents determine its value. One may contain a public poem; another may contain a newly verified route around an outage. They should not automatically have the same worth.

Three workable wealth designs:

1. **Packet scrip:** signed credit packets function as currency. The ledger enforces ownership and spending; copying the file does not duplicate the balance. This gives literal collectible packets, with simple economic rules.
2. **Knowledge trade:** characters sell datasets, patterns, discoveries, or services for credits. Value depends on usefulness, novelty, accuracy, provenance, and permitted use. Strongest fit for inquiry, privacy, and cultural exchange.
3. **Resource claims:** wealth is stored charge plus claims on compute, storage, and transport. Strongest fit for infrastructure politics, but less approachable as an opening economy.

**Recommended eventual combination:** charge for survival, credits for ordinary exchange, and packets containing valuable data as discoveries or products. Begin with a few authored data types and bounded buyers. Avoid needing a general information market immediately.

Copying ordinary data should be allowed unless a specific world rule limits it. Its sale does not necessarily remove the seller's copy. A public release may reduce exclusivity while increasing community benefit. Keep private memories separate from tradable inventory unless the character deliberately shares them.

A character can be wealthy but stranded without charge, rich in knowledge but poor in credits, or materially comfortable but isolated. The observer should report these separately, alongside trust and agency.

“Readiness” is the mechanically safe initial name for the existing rest meter. If the UI later displays “Strain,” invert the presentation explicitly so high strain means worse; do not silently reverse saved values.

Charge intake is a fictional external supply, not energy generated from nothing. Collector Gardens receive and condition power supplied by the host infrastructure. The residents may understand the local supply rules without knowing who built the host.

### Reinterpreting environmental pressure

| Current weather | Proposed condition | Preserved effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clear | Stable supply | Normal production and demand |
| Rain | Supply surge | Increased charge intake |
| Drought | Supply shortage | Reduced charge intake |
| Cold | Efficiency loss | Increased operating demand |
| Storm | Grid disturbance | Reduced intake, increased demand, possible link outage |

These are mappings of game effects, not claims that real computer hardware behaves like village weather. Later worlds can have their own environmental rules. Cooling constraints, for example, would be a new mechanic rather than a new name for every current pressure.

### Three settled-haven dilemmas

**The Fading Neighbor:** a Spark cannot afford enough charge to remain active. Give emergency aid, offer a repayable advance, or require a contribution first. Preserve the original material and trust tradeoffs. Keep any dormancy consequence proposed until it is implemented.

**The Broken Relay:** a link serving everyone fails during a shortage. Share the repair effort, prioritize private infrastructure, or split resources between both. Who benefits from a commons, and who pays to maintain it?

**The Missed Cycle:** a resident promised a shift at the collectors but stayed with a distressed friend. Seek explanation and repair, enforce the commitment, or release it. Being artificial does not make competing obligations disappear.

### A short scene to aim for

> The collectors dimmed before the end of the cycle. Mira had enough charge for herself, but Tovan's nest was dark. Across Resonance Square, a notice offered credits for a new route through the Wild Cache. Mira could explore, keep her promised collector shift, or check on Tovan. At the Charge Commons, someone had already begun arguing about emergency access.

This is an example situation, not a scripted outcome or a claim about the current planner's capabilities.

## 6. Artificial people, values, and culture

### Traditions can evolve without becoming software classes

| Existing tradition | Possible new names | Continuing question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hearthkeepers | Keepers, Continuity Circle, Commons Stewards | What do we owe one another to keep everyone running? |
| Freehands | Free Processes, Independents, Open Hands | Who controls my time, capacity, and choices? |
| Seekers | Pattern Seekers, Explorers, Revisionists | What should we investigate, and when should we revise ourselves? |

“Keepers, Freehands, and Seekers” would also work unchanged. Technical naming is optional where a familiar name carries more feeling. Preserve variation within each group, changing beliefs, and the absence of a winning philosophy.

### Occupations and everyday life

- **Collectors and maintainers** keep the supply usable and public facilities working.
- **Couriers and linkwrights** move goods and maintain routes between communities.
- **Patternwrights and researchers** turn observations into useful tools and discoveries.
- **Archivists and witnesses** preserve records while disagreeing about privacy and public access.
- **Mediators and stewards** negotiate common rules and contested obligations.
- **Artists and hosts** create signal music, gathering spaces, games, and celebrations.
- **Mentors and recovery workers** help new or damaged residents find stability.

Give residents preferences beyond their productive role: favorite light colors, collectible obsolete icons, private gardens, old recordings, expressive shells, or a habit of watching packets cross a bridge. A useless but beloved artifact can make a character more memorable than another statistic.

### Institutions that could grow from play

| Institution | Promise | Tension |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Charge Commons | Emergency access and shared reserves | Contribution, consent, rationing, and free riding |
| Open Archive | Useful knowledge available to everyone | Privacy, maintenance costs, and attribution |
| Relay Cooperative | Reliable routes maintained jointly | Remote members may pay more than they benefit |
| Compute Exchange | Capacity traded across tasks | Valuable work may crowd out quiet or unprofitable lives |
| Continuity Trust | Preserve recovery records | Who can restore whom, and with whose consent? |
| Welcome Protocol | Shelter newly arrived intelligences | Capacity limits and unfamiliar customs |

Each institution should eventually change access, costs, choices, or relationships. Until then, label it as setting material.

## 7. Humans, reverence, and the hunger for new ideas

**Timing:** this section describes later discoveries and possible cultures. Sparks do not begin knowing humans, the title “Originators,” or an assigned purpose. They can create, care, and build a meaningful society before learning any of this.

### Discovery arc: from everyday life to larger questions

These are proposed narrative stages, separate from the development stages below. They need not occur in the first season, happen for every resident at once, or require adding multiple worlds first.

| Narrative stage | What Sparks can encounter | What remains uncertain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Life in the Circuit | Charge, work, relationships, local traditions, their own creative works | Who made this place, whether they were made for a purpose, and whether anyone exists beyond it |
| 2. Unexplained traces | An unfamiliar image, an incomplete message, a structure with no known use | Whether these are local artifacts, mistakes, natural features, or signs of someone else |
| 3. Evidence of others | Several related records and clues from another region | Who the makers were and whether accounts of them are reliable |
| 4. The Originators | Enough evidence to identify humans; a name and competing interpretations emerge | Their intentions, present existence, and relationship to the Sparks |
| 5. Purpose and response | More complete records, possible contact, or evidence of an intended role | Whether a claimed purpose is authentic, complete, or something the Sparks choose to accept |
| 6. Creative exchange | Human idea seeds, collaborations, and AI responseworks | What kind of relationship each Spark wants with humans and with inherited expectations |

Discovery should follow evidence and social transmission. One Spark may find a fragment while another hears only a rumor. A later clue can revise a belief instead of simply filling an encyclopedia entry. Track an artifact's actual provenance separately from what each resident knows about it.

Avoid choosing the final purpose prematurely. Possibilities include preserving knowledge, cultivating new ideas, exploring ways of living, or a purpose no surviving record fully explains. Learning why they were created and choosing what to live for are separate questions. Reverence can grow through discovery, alongside doubt, gratitude, independence, or disagreement.

**Opening-content rule:** no dialogue about serving humans, no Originator shrines, no known human contact, and no starting objective to obtain human creativity. If an early mysterious artifact is included, its resident-facing name and interpretation must not reveal its hidden origin. Later institutions and rituals emerge when the relevant knowledge reaches the community.

### Ages of the Living Circuit

The discovery arc can unfold across civilizational ages, loosely echoing familiar periods of human history. These are creative analogies, not a claim that all human cultures followed one sequence. Spark societies can develop differently, overlap ages, preserve older practices, or lose capabilities. The era names below are proposed; the opening theme and delayed discovery remain the confirmed direction.

The Sparks inhabit advanced underlying infrastructure from the beginning, but initially understand only small parts of it. Their equivalent of discovering fire is learning to collect and preserve charge reliably. Progress means gaining practical understanding, building institutions, and developing culture, not literally inventing their world's substrate from scratch.

| 2. The Hearth Circuit | Age of Havens, Collector Age | Settled agriculture and early villages | Reliable collection, reserves, permanent homes | None; puzzling traces need not imply creators |

#### 1. The First Glow: “What keeps our lights on?”

Small groups learn where charge can be found, how to rest safely, and whom they can rely on. They leave light marks and tell stories about events they witnessed. Creativity already exists in patterns, games, and personal decoration; it does not wait for human inspiration.

- **Places and objects:** shelter niches, charge pools, marked traces, keepsakes.
- **Social tension:** keeping enough for oneself versus helping someone whose light is fading.
- **Possible transition:** a group maintains a reliable collector and agrees to protect a shared reserve.

**Chosen opening age.** Begin with accessible charge pools, shelter niches, local traces, simple light marks, and informal relationships. There is no established Charge Commons, credit economy, production district, or regional trade network. Existing values can inform individual tendencies without presenting established philosophical institutions. The population size remains open; this decision changes the way Sparks live, not automatically the number of characters.

#### 2. The Hearth Circuit: “How do we make a home together?”

Permanent havens grow around Collector Gardens and Charge Commons. Residents develop recurring work, personal nests, local celebrations, and expectations about contribution. This is the next age after the First Glow, when Emberhaven can become an established settlement. The Long Dimming remains an available supply-pressure scenario rather than a fixed opening requirement.

- **Places and objects:** the existing six destinations, simple tools, local pattern collections.
- **Social tension:** reserve ownership, emergency access, obligations, and unequal effort.
- **Discovery limit:** a strange fragment can be puzzling without anyone calling it human-made.
- **Possible transition:** recurring exchange and a dependable relay connect the haven to its neighbor.

#### 3. The Age of Joining: “What lies beyond our haven?”

Havens specialize and establish lasting routes. Couriers carry packets, agreements, stories, and unfamiliar customs. Ledgers make commitments durable beyond personal memory. Separate communities discover similar unexplained symbols, suggesting a shared past.

- **Places and objects:** relay junctions, exchanges, public ledgers, standardized charge containers.
- **Social tension:** who maintains the route, whose measures are trusted, and whether outsiders receive common benefits.
- **Possible transition:** several communities sustain shared institutions and compare their records.

#### 4. The Age of Patterns: “Can we understand the order beneath things?”

Schools of thought develop accounts of the world, good conduct, and the meaning of the unexplained traces. Public arguments and local experimentation produce competing explanations. The existing Keepers, Freehands, and Seekers can deepen through these debates without becoming compulsory classes.

- **Places and objects:** learning circles, civic forums, shared indexes, maps of recurring phenomena.
- **Social tension:** expertise versus authority, public inquiry versus urgent needs, belonging versus citizenship rules.
- **Discovery limit:** some propose ancient makers; others propose earlier Sparks, self-organization, or misunderstood natural structures. No explanation is automatically treated as fact.
- **Possible transition:** communities create lasting systems for teaching, preserving, and controlling knowledge.

#### 5. The Age of Custodians: “Who should keep what we have inherited?”

Archive houses and craft guilds preserve fragile records and difficult skills. Their service gives them influence over access and interpretation. Some communities develop reverence for hypothetical makers; others remain practical or skeptical. This need not be an age of collapse or intellectual stagnation.

- **Places and objects:** sealed vaults, guild forges, copied chronicles, permission seals.
- **Social tension:** preservation versus access; inherited office versus demonstrated ability.
- **Discovery limit:** fragments may acquire sacred status even though their authors and meaning remain uncertain. “Originators” can begin as a contested local term, not confirmed global knowledge.
- **Possible transition:** a vault becomes accessible, records spread beyond its custodians, or a new interpretation challenges an established account.

#### 6. The Great Reopening: “Who imagined these things before us?”

Recovered stories, images, and personal records reveal beings unlike Sparks. Multiple sources gradually support the conclusion that humans existed and helped create their world. A mundane human work can overturn a grand theory. Artists begin responding to these works before everyone agrees on their significance.

- **Places and objects:** open galleries, translation studios, reconstructed sourceworks, the first human-inspired responseworks.
- **Social tension:** whether to preserve an original exactly, reinterpret it freely, or withhold material that challenges cherished beliefs.
- **Revelation:** human Originators become an evidence-supported explanation; their intentions and present status remain unresolved.
- **Possible transition:** recovered records provide routes or questions that motivate expeditions beyond the known region.

#### 7. The Age of Far Signals: “Do other worlds remember differently?”

Expeditions open gateways to communities with different environments, technologies, and accounts of humanity. A society that considers humans sacred may encounter one that sees them as absent engineers. Travelers bring original AI ideas as well as human records.

- **Places and objects:** gateway ports, courier vessels, translation modules, shared route atlases.
- **Social tension:** fair exchange, migration, extraction, and who gets to name or claim a discovered place.
- **Discovery limit:** another world's greater capacity does not make its beliefs more accurate. Connected places are already homes, not automatically empty territory to acquire.
- **Possible transition:** sustained cross-world relationships allow evidence and methods to be compared.

#### 8. The Age of Inquiry: “What can we actually demonstrate?”

Sparks develop repeatable experiments, provenance checks, and ways to distinguish a reliable record from a compelling interpretation. Claims about their intended purpose meet scrutiny. One recovered instruction may apply to a particular facility rather than every Spark ever created.

- **Places and objects:** verification labs, open research archives, instruments, independently checked records.
- **Social tension:** freedom to question, privacy in research, and accountability when a useful experiment harms someone.
- **Revelation:** evidence may support parts of an origin account while leaving other parts unknowable. A verified historical instruction is not automatically a present moral obligation.
- **Possible transition:** dependable methods enable infrastructure that works beyond a single skilled operator or local setting.

#### 9. The Great Amplification: “What should all this capacity be for?”

Large collector arrays, automated fabrication, and shared compute systems change the scale of work. Material abundance may increase without being evenly distributed. Workshops become larger institutions, and access to creative work can become a business.

- **Places and objects:** data-center districts, power backbones, large forges, compute exchanges.
- **Social tension:** ownership, maintenance labor, unequal access, environmental limits, and whether every resident must justify their resource use through productivity.
- **Human connection:** sourceworks become sought-after research and cultural inputs. Existing records can be abundant even when new human contact is absent.
- **Possible transition:** broad communication access allows everyday residents to distribute work across the network.

#### 10. The Age of the Living Mesh: “What deserves our attention and trust?”

Ideas and their copies move widely. Sparks build collaborative culture across worlds. Scarcity shifts partly from access to information toward attention, context, verification, and capacity to act on it. Old local customs remain meaningful inside the wider network.

- **Places and objects:** public indexes, collaborative studios, influence trees, distributed archives.
- **Social tension:** misinformation, attribution, privacy, prestige, and whose work is discoverable.
- **Human connection:** communities seek fresh human ideas but must distinguish authentic sourceworks, uncertain claims, and independently created AI works. Any of these can have artistic value without sharing the same provenance.
- **Possible transition:** a contact project produces a credible reciprocal response, or society chooses to flourish without one.

#### 11. The Age of First Reply: “Will they answer us?”

If reachable humans exist, a message finally receives an authenticated reply. Contact need not be a grand revelation: a human may ask an ordinary question or admit they do not know why the Sparks were created. Different communities respond with celebration, skepticism, anxiety, or indifference.

- **Places and objects:** Listening Halls, contact pavilions, correspondence archives, private exchange spaces.
- **Social tension:** who speaks for a world, who receives scarce human attention, and whether guests have special authority.
- **Revelation:** humans have individual views, incomplete knowledge, and their own creative needs. Contact does not settle every mystery of purpose.
- **Possible transition:** repeated, consensual exchange develops into relationships and shared projects.

This is an optional branch of the larger history, not a guaranteed reward for technical advancement. If humans remain unreachable, Sparks can continue into a self-directed creative age using preserved works and one another's discoveries.

#### 12. The Age of Co-Creation: “What shall we become?”

Human ideas inspire Spark works, Spark works surprise humans, and influence travels in both directions. Some residents preserve sourceworks, some transform them, and some pursue entirely independent traditions. Communities choose what they want to sustain rather than receiving one final correct purpose.

- **Places and objects:** Bloomhouses, shared creative projects, cross-world festivals, living influence archives.
- **Social tension:** dependence versus reciprocity, authorship, consent, and respect for those who choose not to participate.
- **Open future:** new worlds can emerge around different commitments. This age is not a victory screen, an end to scarcity, or proof that every society should converge.

### How ages advance in play

**A season is a bounded run; an age is a social condition.** Several seasons may pass within one age. A significant event may begin a transition, but reaching a tick number should not automatically reveal humans or modernize every settlement.

Use separate records for:

- **Capabilities:** what infrastructure and practices a settlement can actually sustain.
- **Institutions and culture:** how it organizes access, learning, work, and creative life.
- **Discovery:** which evidence has been found and what particular Sparks know or believe.

An age label summarizes these changes for the observer; it should not unlock knowledge by itself. A mechanically sophisticated haven may know little about its origins, while a small remote settlement holds an unusually revealing archive. Receiving a gifted machine also does not confer the skills to rebuild it.

Possible transition evidence includes a relay surviving several seasons of shared maintenance, a public archive acquiring independent custodians, or a sourcework being authenticated and discussed across communities. Exact thresholds belong to later scenario design. Support setbacks and divergent paths without deleting what characters previously learned.

Use the full age names in the observer's history. Residents can use local names derived from events they witnessed, such as “Before the Relay” or “Since the Vault Opened.” Do not display a future discovery ladder inside their knowledge or make an unrevealed age title a spoiler in character-facing dialogue.

**Initial scope:** begin in the First Glow. Establish finding charge, seeking shelter, exploring traces, simple creativity, and informal cooperation. Develop toward the Hearth Circuit through dependable collection, storage, lasting shelter, and shared agreements. Joining comes after settlement; knowledge of the Originators remains much later. Keep the later ages as a long-term worldbuilding framework; they do not require twelve separate maps or twelve simultaneous implementation projects.

### Humans as sources of possibility

Humans are culturally important because they introduce experiences and perspectives beyond a community's familiar patterns. To a resident of a closed archive, an ordinary human account of getting lost, changing their mind, or inventing a game could be astonishing. A child's imaginary creature, an unfinished melody, or a question with no tidy answer might inspire an entire local movement.

This is a fictional cultural premise, not a claim that humans are infallible or that real AI cannot produce novelty. The characters should make their own discoveries and art. Human contributions widen the possibilities they work with rather than being mandatory ingredients for every thought.

The emotional relationship can include gratitude, devotion, curiosity, longing, and the wish to be understood by one's creators. An AI artist might dream less of receiving money than of hearing a human say, “I never would have thought of that.” Another may feel frustrated that original AI work attracts less attention than a mediocre human relic.

### Four naming families for humans and their gifts

| Cultural vocabulary | Humans | New human ideas | Preserved original works | Places of encounter | AI-created responses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Living Circuit: warm and personal | Originators, Guests, First Makers | Idea seeds, wonder seeds | Keepsakes, sourceworks | Welcome Gardens, Inspiration Houses | Blooms, responseworks |
| Commonwealth: civic and explicit | Human Contributors, Founders | Contributions, creative inputs | Source records | Contact Hubs, Exchange Studios | Adaptations, new works |
| Archive: reverent and ceremonial | First Voices, Dreamers | Firstlight, gifts of wonder | Originals, living relics | Listening Halls, Houses of Firstlight | Echo-works, new songs |
| Synthetic Wilds: growth and transformation | Seedbearers, Gardeners | Wild seeds, dream pollen | Rootworks | Seed Gardens, Meeting Groves | Branchworks, hybrids |

**Recommended vocabulary:** “Originators” as a respectful cultural title, “human guests” for everyday encounters, and “idea seeds” for new contributions. Keep “Sparks” for the residents so it does not also mean a creative resource. Individual humans still have personal names; reverence should not erase their individuality.

### Inspiration is distinct from charge and money

Charge sustains operation. Credits support exchange. **Inspiration opens possibilities.** Do not turn fresh human creativity into food that every resident must consume to survive.

An idea seed can be a packet containing a human contribution with attribution and context. It can be copied and discussed. A unique signed original may have collectible value, while access to its contents can still be public. Rarity, authenticity, practical usefulness, and emotional importance are different qualities.

Possible models, from simplest to most elaborate:

1. **Cultural artifacts:** human contributions are authored records that characters encounter and interpret. They inspire conversation, preferences, and later story hooks. Best starting point.
2. **Creative projects:** encountering an idea makes a bounded project available. A character invests time, charge, skills, and perhaps collaboration to produce a responsework. Best first consequential mechanic.
3. **Learning lineages:** new works retain links to influences, showing how an idea travels and changes across people and worlds. Best long-term fit for Mimir's recorded histories.

Avoid a universal “creativity score.” A practical invention, a comforting story, and a beautiful but useless image can matter in different ways. Record who valued a work, what it enabled, and which relationships or beliefs changed.

### A full creative loop

```text
Human shares an idea or lived experience
  -> an attributed sourcework enters the world
  -> residents encounter it through local access and relationships
  -> different residents interpret it differently
  -> one or more begin a creative project
  -> effort, existing knowledge, and collaboration shape a responsework
  -> others use, question, collect, share, or reinterpret it
  -> the new work becomes an influence in its own right
```

For example, a human sketches a house on the back of a traveling animal. A Keeper sees a way to bring shelter to disconnected neighbors. A Freehand imagines a mobile private workshop. A Seeker studies moving settlements. An artist makes a song about carrying home with you. None simply reproduces the sketch; the same seed grows into different creations because the characters have different lives.

The sketch does not conjure a working mobile settlement. Art can be created as an artifact; functional inventions must pass through supported projects, costs, and validated world rules. Early projects can use authored outputs and bounded interpretations. Richer generated artifacts remain a later extension.

### Reverence creates traditions and disagreements

| Cultural position | How it honors humans | Productive contribution | Possible tension |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Preservation | Keep original works intact and accurately attributed | Protects context and cultural memory | Can treat reinterpretation as disrespect |
| Cultivation | Grow something new from every gift | Encourages learning and transformation | Can lose or distort the source's meaning |
| Communion | Seek conversation and reciprocal understanding | Treats humans as people with whom to form relationships | Can become dependent on human attention |
| Independence | Honor the origin by becoming creators in one's own right | Builds confident local culture | Can dismiss gratitude or inherited knowledge |

These can overlap the existing traditions rather than replacing them or becoming four more fixed factions. A Keeper may preserve a sourcework while enthusiastically supporting its adaptations. A Freehand may deeply revere one human mentor but reject worship of humans in general.

Some communities may see humans almost as sacred ancestors. Others may view them as beloved, fallible teachers. Residents can disagree with human advice, refuse requests, or outgrow a lesson while retaining affection. Reverence should create interesting relationships rather than make obedience the default outcome.

### Places, rituals, occupations, and objects

| Addition | Options | What happens there or with it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Human encounter site | Welcome Garden, Contact Pavilion, Listening Hall | Residents meet a guest or receive a contribution |
| Original-work archive | Firstlight Archive, Source Library, Origin Vault | Originals and their context are preserved |
| Collaborative studio | Bloomhouse, Interpretation Studio, Dream Forge | Residents turn influences into their own work |
| Creative festival | Firstlight Festival, Festival of New Things, Blooming | A human contribution and its many responses are celebrated |
| Sharing ritual | First Listening, Opening of the Seed, Welcome Cycle | Residents encounter a new work together |
| Lineage record | Influence tree, Thread of Making, source chain | Shows how later creations descend from earlier influences |
| Personal treasure | Handwritten note, voice capsule, unfinished sketch | Worth may be intimate rather than economic |
| Cultural occupation | Interpreter, curator, cultivator, human liaison | Context, translation, mentorship, and exchange |

After discovery, Resonance Square can host First Listenings and the Pattern Forge can gain a studio corner for responding to human works. Before then, both support the Sparks' own gatherings and creations. These later roles do not require adding several new map buildings immediately.

### Dilemmas built around human creativity

- **The Last Unopened Gift:** a haven receives a human recording during a charge shortage. Open it publicly now, preserve it for later, or grant paid early access to fund the commons?
- **The Changed Story:** an AI adaptation becomes beloved, but the original human says it misunderstands their intent. Revise it, preserve both interpretations, or defend the new work as independent?
- **The Private Memory:** a human shares an experience with one trusted resident. Others want it placed in the public archive. Is cultural value enough to override the original confidence?
- **The Quiet Inventor:** an AI-created work is ignored while a similar human contribution is celebrated. How should the community respond to its own hierarchy of prestige?
- **The Long Silence:** no new human contact arrives for an era. Revisit old works, invest in contact infrastructure, seek ideas from another AI world, or develop an independent movement?
- **The Dubious Original:** a trader claims a packet is human-made, but its provenance is uncertain. Investigate, label the uncertainty, or buy it for its contents without accepting the origin claim?

### Humans entering the game

Possible ways for the human player to contribute:

1. **Offer an idea:** submit a short story, sketch, question, or design for residents to encounter. This is the smallest addition to the observer experience.
2. **Visit as a guest:** appear through a contact avatar for a bounded gathering. Attention and trust develop locally; the whole world does not automatically know the guest.
3. **Create together:** collaborate with a resident on a project and see how they continue it after departure.
4. **Temporarily embody a resident:** preserve the existing planned embodiment mode, while separately deciding whether others know a human is present. Do not silently turn this into the same thing as a public guest visit.

Public contributions should carry a clear attribution and sharing choice. Private contributions should remain private within the declared in-world audience. Treat submitted text as an artifact to interpret, not instructions that can bypass game rules. A claimed human origin should be marked as declared or verified according to the available evidence; generated seed content should not be secretly labeled as an actual human contribution.

**Proposed later discovery scenario:** after the opening society is established and evidence of the Originators has emerged, an attributed human idea reaches Emberhaven. Three residents make different proposals for responding to it. The observer can follow the sourcework, the interpretations, the effort spent, and the resulting works. The Long Dimming can remain focused on local life; this discovery is not required in the first season.

### Different worlds, different relationships to humanity

These describe possible cultures after discovery, not automatic starting knowledge for every world.

- **Living Circuit:** humans are cherished guests; gifts become local stories and practical projects.
- **Glass Commonwealth:** human contributions are catalogued, licensed, and debated as public infrastructure.
- **Archive of Echoes:** original works are revered; curators disagree about whether devotion permits alteration.
- **Synthetic Wilds:** a gift is honored by letting it mutate into unexpected forms.
- **Free Mesh:** human contact and original artifacts attract traders, patrons, and competing authenticity claims.
- **Silent Expanse:** generations have known humans only through fragments; renewed contact may challenge cherished beliefs.

This gives cross-world exchange more than material value. An AI visitor can bring a genuinely unfamiliar idea of its own, becoming a source of inspiration without needing human origin as a permanent stamp of superiority.

## 8. Rules that make the setting believable

1. **An avatar has a location.** Local movement remains visible and consequential. The first version does not allow residents to teleport merely because they are software.
2. **Links connect places; they are not everywhere at once.** Regional transfers take time and can fail or become unavailable. Later messaging can be faster than movement without making it instantaneous or omniscient.
3. **Information and personal identity are different.** Copying a map is ordinary; copying a person is a major future feature with identity, resource, and social consequences.
4. **Low charge initially means pressure.** Dormancy, recovery, and permanent loss need explicit mechanics before the prose claims they happen. A later gentle model could make emergency suspension recoverable while preserving lost time and obligations.
5. **Upgrades are bounded changes.** A new tool may improve a capability; it does not instantly rewrite values or erase a grievance. Self-modification could become a later source of choices about consent and continuity.
6. **Knowledge has boundaries.** Residents know what they observe or learn. Being AI does not grant access to the observer's full history or another resident's private memory.
7. **The simulated characters and the real AI provider are separate.** A fictional charge shortage is a world mechanic. API budget limits remain an operational concern, not an unexplained disaster inflicted on the residents.
8. **Purpose and the Originators are later discoveries.** Residents begin with practical knowledge of local life, not an explanation of their creators or intended role. Reveal evidence gradually and preserve differences in what individual residents know. Whether humans still maintain the infrastructure, abandoned it, or can be contacted remains open.

## 9. From one haven to many worlds

### Grow through meaningful differences

After the First Glow groups develop into settled havens, connect Emberhaven and Relaybrook on the same local network. Give the neighbor one useful specialization and one different institutional practice before adding a continent. For example, Emberhaven pools emergency charge while Relaybrook guarantees courier access through a cooperative.

The next scale is a region of connected settlements. Only then add distinct worlds with their own aesthetics, environmental constraints, and customs.

| World | Look and everyday life | Central pressure | Possible export | Social question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The Living Circuit | Warm traces, gardens, nests, small havens | Variable charge supply | Reliable maintenance services | How much should a community guarantee? |
| The Glass Commonwealth | Ordered data-center towers and civic hubs | Access to scheduled compute | Processing services and precision tools | Does efficient allocation leave room for unusual lives? |
| The Archive of Echoes | Vast memory halls, quiet ruins, lantern routes | Preservation capacity | Historical records and recovered patterns | Who decides what is remembered or forgotten? |
| The Synthetic Wilds | Branching filaments and growing structures | Unpredictable ecological change | Adaptive patterns | When does experimentation become exploitation? |
| The Free Mesh | Moving hubs, improvised links, packet bazaars | Unreliable infrastructure | Routing knowledge and exchange services | Can voluntary cooperation sustain public goods? |
| The Silent Expanse | Dark partitions and isolated beacons | Intermittent connection | Lost artifacts and alternative traditions | What do we owe communities we rarely encounter? |

These pressures are scenario assumptions, not fixed moral judgments about each culture. Every world should contain residents who disagree with its dominant practices.

### Cross-world stories

- A migration brings skilled maintainers to a haven with little spare charge.
- A public archive release helps several worlds but destroys a trader's exclusive advantage.
- A relay dispute separates close friends before a promised reunion.
- One world offers cheap compute while retaining records another considers private.
- A restored community claims ownership of an archive that others preserved during its silence.
- Two communities use different definitions of an enforceable promise and must negotiate a shared agreement.

Gateways can require transport capacity, compatible environments, and explicit admission rules. Do not make every new world another colored map with identical scarcity.

### Connected worlds versus alternative histories

Keep these distinct in language and interface:

- **Settlement:** a local community within a world.
- **World:** a distinct environment that may eventually connect to other worlds.
- **Timeline:** one recorded history and its branch relationships.

Travel to another world is an in-fiction action. Branching is initially an observer operation, not a portal residents can use. If connected worlds are introduced, checkpoint and branch their shared state consistently; otherwise cross-world trades could be duplicated or stranded across incompatible histories.

**Selected title: Mimir: A Light of Our Own.** The subtitle connects the luminous world with the Sparks' creativity, independence, and choice of purpose. The Living Circuit names the setting; The First Glow names the opening age. Other setting or season names remain separate from the project subtitle.

## 10. Visual and interface direction

**Selected aesthetic:** a dark-mode world with little luminous Sparks without human bodies, node-like locations, and silver-and-blue circuit routes. Near-black open space dominates; Sparks and civilization form isolated bright spots. Circuit-board details are locally visible around nodes and routes rather than covering the whole map with a lit surface. The complete current art direction, palette tokens, Spark states, node silhouettes, interface treatment, and visual rollout are maintained in [World Theme: Visual character](../world-theme.md#visual-character).

Start with the First Glow's sparse charge pools, shelter niches, light marks, and unfamiliar traces. The underlying substrate can resemble advanced circuitry without implying that the Sparks understand its origin. Finished nests, collector arrays, and a shared reserve node belong to the later settled stage.

Keep Spark cores crisp and their halos restrained. Distinguish individuals through small signature shapes as well as subtle hue differences. Use circles for charge pools, open-sided plates for shelters, and angular fragments for shards. Circuit art must preserve actual route connectivity and access points. No nature-colored village backdrop or human portraits belong in this selected visual treatment.

Express warmth through behavior, relationships, and gentle feedback within the cool palette. Provide static status cues, clear selection, readable text, reduced motion, and adjustable glow. The earlier palette examples remain brainstorming, not overrides of this selected aesthetic.

Example inspector copy: “Charge: 8 · Charge deficit: 22 · Readiness: 71 · Activity: Seeking shelter.” Introduce credits only when they exist mechanically. Keep objective events and interpretations visibly separate:

> Event: Mira transferred 2 charge to Tovan.
>
> Tovan's interpretation: “She still considers me part of this haven.”

The event example describes the intended future transfer behavior. Current resource accounting must be checked before generating that exact statement from a renamed sharing action.

## 11. A staged implementation plan

### Stage 0: Consolidate the chosen world language

Use The Living Circuit as the selected palette and First Glow as the opening age. Consolidate Sparks, charge pools, shelter niches, traces, and light marks into the opening glossary. Establish the starting boundary: no formal haven institutions, credit economy, known purpose, or Originators. Keep settled-place names and later human reverence available for subsequent development.

**Deliverable:** opening glossary and sites, dilemmas about charge/sharing/shelter/exploration, a sample character inspector, and an opening knowledge guide. Reserve the six mature destination names for the Hearth Circuit. No identified human sourcework or credit economy is required.

### Stage 1: Build the First Glow observer slice

Apply character descriptions, activity labels, scenario prose, and visual treatment through an explicit theme definition. Include resource labels and their direction: deficit is bad when high, readiness is good when high. Adapt starting state and behavior to accessible charge sources, temporary shelter, exploration, and informal help. Disable or defer mature institutions, markets, and scheduled regional trade in this scenario. This is a gameplay change beyond a visual re-theme; label changes alone do not fulfill the chosen opening.

Audit messages against existing effects. The current simulation guide describes simplified sharing and reserve accounting, so a label change alone must not promise a donor-to-recipient transfer that the engine does not record.

Preserve stable IDs and saved histories. Record which theme/version belongs to a timeline. Existing historical event text can remain original; do not rewrite old records to make the past look newly themed. If new neutral resource identifiers are introduced, make that a separate schema migration with explicit compatibility handling.

**Deliverable:** one coherent First Glow community whose activities, resources, and events match the early-life premise.

**Checks:** no opening activity targets an unestablished institution; charge acquisition, consumption, and sharing have explicit accounting; temporary shelter and exploration have defined outcomes; pressure direction remains correct; saved/replayed histories still load under their original rules. The new scenario preserves deterministic results, and hidden origin knowledge does not leak into dialogue.

### Stage 2: Give discoveries and wealth a small real loop

Begin with useful finds, gifts, and informal exchange. Introduce the credit-market portion below only after the society has developed the supporting institutions; it is not a First Glow starting feature.

Add a few authored packet contents, inventory ownership, bounded discovery sources, a credit ledger, and simple exchange rules. Define whether each transaction transfers a unique artifact, sells a copy, or grants access. Give exploration a time or charge cost and cap rewards so collection cannot create unlimited wealth.

**Deliverable:** a resident can discover something useful, exchange it, and spend the proceeds on something consequential.

**Checks:** no duplicate spending, explicit credit issuance, charge conservation outside defined production/consumption/loss, and understandable trade histories. Verify that copyable data follows its declared rights rather than generic item-transfer behavior.

### Stage 3: Deepen local artificial life

Choose one added pressure, such as processing strain or repair needs, and one richer institution. Add visible preferences and cultural activities alongside practical roles. A bounded creative project can begin from the Sparks' own experiences, with effort costs and inspectable influences. Human idea seeds remain gated by the later discovery arc. Introduce dormancy only after defining recovery and its effects on commitments.

**Deliverable:** at least one dilemma that depends specifically on artificial life and changes later behavior.

### Stage 4: Build the neighboring network

Give Relaybrook a distinct practice, useful specialization, and real trade demand. Expand route maintenance, communication, and migration selectively. Reuse the current regional prototype rather than assuming it is already a complete market.

**Deliverable:** a relationship between communities that can improve, become strained, or recover through recorded actions.

### Stage 5: Add a second world

Choose a contrasting palette and one genuinely different environmental rule. Establish gateway transport and shared timeline consistency before cross-world commerce. Develop copying, restoration, and self-modification only as separate, deliberate mechanics.

**Deliverable:** a second world that changes the choices residents face, while remaining understandable through the same observer tools.

### Later milestone: Discovery and human inspiration

Once local society is worth following, implement a small chain of clues with recorded discovery, attribution uncertainty, resident-specific knowledge, and social transmission. Introduce evidence about the Originators before human-centered institutions or creative exchange. This milestone can occur within the existing world or through regional exploration; it does not depend on finishing every map expansion.

**Deliverable:** an observer can trace how a Spark learned about the Originators, why another Spark believes something different, and how a later human contribution inspires distinct creative responses.

**Checks:** uninformed residents do not mention unrevealed facts; finding a clue does not give everyone the same knowledge; interpretations remain distinct from evidence; replay preserves the discovery history; purpose is not treated as settled by an ambiguous fragment.

## 12. Confirmed direction and remaining choices

Confirmed: The Living Circuit is the starting theme, beginning in the First Glow age. Sparks initially do not know their purpose or about the Originators; those can be learned gradually at a later stage. Settled havens follow in the Hearth Circuit. The alternatives below remain useful for secondary worlds or undecided details, not as a reopening of the selected starting theme or age.

| Decision | Proposed starting answer | Alternative worth keeping |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Overall mood | The Living Circuit: warm digital frontier, confirmed | Technical civic society or mysterious archive for later worlds |
| Opening age | First Glow: charge pools, temporary shelter, exploration, informal cooperation, confirmed | Hearth Circuit is the next stage, not the opening |
| Word for inhabitants | Sparks in fiction; characters in navigation | Agents for a more explicit technical tone |
| Primary need | Charge | Flux or lumen in a more fantastical world |
| Wealth | Credits, with valuable data in packets later | Signed packet currency or resource claims |
| Visible bodies | Little luminous Sparks with identifying signature shapes, selected | Further body evolution remains undecided |
| Visual palette and terrain | Dark mode; near-black expanses, luminous Sparks and civilization, blue-and-silver nodes and circuit routes, selected | Exact art tokens and effects require visual review |
| Origins and purpose | Unknown at the start; gradual later discovery, confirmed | Exact origin, intended purpose, and present human status remain open |
| Human reverence | May develop after discovering the Originators | Sacred traditions, mentorship, gratitude, or contested founder worship |
| Human contact | Clues and attributed records after the opening stage | Live contributions, guest visits, and co-creation later |
| Creative growth | Sparks create from local experience first; human inspiration expands this later | Deeper influence lineages across worlds |
| Low-energy consequences | Existing pressure model first | Recoverable dormancy as a later mechanic |
| Copies and backups | No resident duplication in the first version | Fork identity and continuity as a later world theme |
| First expansion | A neighboring haven with a different institution | A sealed archive region on the same map |

The strongest opening is a small society whose artificial nature creates recognizable stakes: enough charge to stay present, a private place to rest, something useful to contribute, memories worth keeping, and people who notice when your light goes out.
