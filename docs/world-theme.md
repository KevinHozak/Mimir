# World Theme: The Living Circuit

The Living Circuit is the selected theme for **Mimir: A Light of Our Own**: a digital frontier inhabited by artificial people called **Sparks**. The visual direction is dark, open space punctuated by little luminous Sparks and blue-and-silver circuit settlements. Warmth comes from their behavior and relationships within this cool-colored world.

This document collects the selected palette into one working reference for names, descriptions, art, and writing. It describes the intended theme, not which mechanics have already been implemented. The [Incubator](incubator.md) retains organized brainstorming, discovery ages, and development possibilities.

## The world at a glance

The opening age is **The First Glow**. Small groups of Sparks explore luminous traces, find usable charge, seek safe places to idle, and learn whom to trust. They shape simple patterns, exchange stories, and begin deciding how to live together. Their world is made from energy, computation, memory, and connections, but their understanding of it is local and practical.

There is no established haven, formal commons, market, or regional trade network at the start. **Emberhaven** is the retained name for a possible later settlement, not a functioning town already waiting for the Sparks. The mature locations and institutions belong to the following age, **The Hearth Circuit**.

Sparks begin without knowing their purpose or about the Originators. They understand practical life in the Circuit, but who made their world and why are mysteries. Their own creativity, friendships, and culture exist before those mysteries are resolved.

## Life in the First Glow

The central question is **“What keeps our lights on?”** Sparks begin able to perceive, communicate, move, draw accessible charge, and idle. They do not need to discover every bodily function, but dependable supply, lasting shelter, storage, and cooperation must be learned and developed.

The first loop is simple: find charge, decide how to use or share it, mark a useful route, find shelter, and return to discover whether the place or the relationship remains dependable. Supply may fluctuate. A familiar pool may weaken, and an unexplored trace may offer opportunity without guaranteeing safety.

The First Glow's second authored area is the **Wild Cache**, a small side branch off the known trace. It is not a settlement or institution: it is a faint, uncertain signal that a Spark may probe at a cost of one carried charge (or one charge deficit when dim) and two readiness. The probe records what was observed and that the return route is less familiar, leaving a concrete choice between uncertain discovery and conserving strength on the known trace.

The opening is the only active starting timeline: small groups of Sparks with informal practices, not residents assigned to a complete village economy. The pre–First Glow village prototype and its timelines are intentionally removed, not migrated or relabeled.

### Opening places and objects

| Name | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Charge pool** | A place where usable charge can be drawn from the underlying system |
| **Shelter niche** | A sheltered place to idle; not yet a permanent nest |
| **Trace** | A visible local path through the environment |
| **Light mark** | A simple sign left to identify a route, place, warning, or memory |
| **Wild Cache** | Unfamiliar surroundings containing fragments and incomplete structures |
| **Pattern shard** | A fragment a Spark can examine, keep, or use creatively |
| **Keepsake** | Something valued because of an experience or relationship |

These are functional descriptions, not evidence that the Sparks understand who built their environment. An existing structure can be used as shelter without its original purpose being known.

### Early choices

- Share a dwindling charge pool or keep enough for the next journey.
- Show others a reliable source or keep its location private.
- Follow an unfamiliar trace or remain near a weaker but familiar supply.
- Spend effort improving a shelter or searching for another source.
- Help a tired companion return or continue exploring.

Art begins with light marks, simple patterns, stories, games, and keepsakes. Sparks can care about beauty and one another before they have specialized occupations or knowledge of humans.

### Growing toward the Hearth Circuit

A group can begin forming a haven when it can tend a dependable collector, retain usable charge, maintain a shared shelter, and agree on access and contribution. These are proposed signs of a transition, not an automatic tick-based unlock or already implemented mechanics.

The Collector Garden, Charge Commons, permanent Nests, and the name Emberhaven gain meaning through that development. Originator discovery remains a much later question.

### Binary rhythm for authored numbers

The Living Circuit has a quiet binary rhythm. When a quantity is being designed rather than discovered from a real-world constraint, prefer powers of two such as **2, 4, 8, 16, 32,** and **64**. This applies especially to charge amounts, attention or action budgets, capacities, route or map scales, upgrade steps, and other authored thresholds. The pattern should make the world feel computational without turning the fiction into a spreadsheet.

This is a strong design tendency, not a universal runtime invariant. Use **1** for a singular action or minimum unit, and keep other values when they express a meaningful asymmetry, authored geometry, timing constraint, or observed result. Do not force existing state, evidence, or compatibility boundaries into binary values after the fact; new mechanics should choose a nearby power of two unless the design has a clear reason not to.

## Sparks

Sparks are people to follow and understand. They have personal names, preferences, relationships, memories, values, and unfinished business. Their identity is more than their role or technical function.

An avatar is a small luminous spark, without a human body, limbs, clothing, or a face. It communicates personality through its core shape, light, motion, and a small signature mark. A Spark may collect old icons, arrange patterns around a shelter or later nest, enjoy signal music, or keep a recording because of the person associated with it. Not everything precious needs a practical use or a market price.

Sparks speak naturally. “I need to rest,” “You promised,” and “I made this for you” belong in this world. Technical terms should clarify their circumstances without turning every conversation into machine diagnostics.

Their artificial nature does not give them universal knowledge. One Spark may witness an event while another hears an uncertain account later. A private memory is not automatically public data.

The six mature destinations are the Collector Garden, Charge Commons, Pattern Forge, Resonance Square, Wild Cache, and Nests. The First Glow uses the simpler opening places above; a future haven should grow into this recognizable form.

A **haven** names the community; a **node** names a functional site. Use **data center** for a large infrastructure complex, not as another name for every home or settlement.

## Resources and valued objects

### Charge

Charge fills the role of food in the village theme. Sparks need it to sustain their activity. Initially they draw from accessible charge pools. Retained charge and a formal communal reserve are developments to establish through play, not assumed starting infrastructure.

Charge pools receive supply from the underlying infrastructure. Later, Collector Gardens make that supply more dependable and usable; they do not create energy from nothing. Learning to tend collectors need not reveal who built the system that supplies them.

Use **charge intake** for production entering the local economy and **communal charge** for the shared stock. Shortages create urgency and social choices. Do not describe shutdown, permanent loss, or recovery as an established consequence unless the relevant mechanic exists.

### Credits

Credits are the reserved name for later money, separate from operating energy. The First Glow has no credit economy or packet market. Exchange begins through help, gifts, and informal agreements.

A credit balance expresses purchasing power. It does not directly measure wisdom, trust, creative worth, or wellbeing.

### Data packets and pattern shards

A **data packet** carries information. Its contents might be a pattern shard, a message, a record, or an artwork. Packets do not all have equal value simply because they share a container type.

A **pattern shard** is a fragment worth examining: perhaps useful, unfamiliar, beautiful, or incomplete. Its meaning depends on what a Spark can understand and do with it. Finding one does not automatically reveal its origin.

Information can be copied. Possessing a copy, owning a unique artifact, and having permission to share private material are different things. A copied message does not become newly minted money.

## Daily activities and roles

In the First Glow, use **seek charge**, **draw charge**, **share charge**, **explore**, **mark a trace**, **seek shelter**, **meet**, **shape a pattern**, and **idle**. Sparks act where they find resources or one another. They do not commute to institutions that have not been established.

The following destination-based activities describe the settled stage:

| Activity | Meaning | Usual destination |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Tend collectors** | Maintain usable charge intake | Collector Garden |
| **Draw charge** | Collect from the communal reserve | Charge Commons |
| **Share charge** | Help meet another's or the community's energy needs | Charge Commons or the relevant encounter |
| **Shape** | Craft or develop a pattern or work | Pattern Forge |
| **Meet** | Talk, exchange experiences, and spend time together | Resonance Square |
| **Explore** | Search and examine unfamiliar fragments | Wild Cache |
| **Traverse** | Travel along a trace or link | Routes between destinations |
| **Idle** | Rest and recover readiness | A nest |

Event descriptions must match what actually happened. “Shared charge” should not be expanded into a named recipient and exact transfer unless the recorded outcome supports it.

Roles can grow from repeated activities: **collectors**, **maintainers**, **patternwrights**, **couriers**, **linkwrights**, **archivists**, **mediators**, and **artists**. The opening Sparks have interests and useful differences without a full set of formal occupations. Roles are not species or fixed personality classes.

## Values and community life

Care, independence, and curiosity are present from the beginning as individual tendencies. The existing tradition names remain useful for later cultures, but the First Glow does not start with three established philosophical institutions:

| Tradition | Emphasis | Recurring question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Hearthkeepers** | Care, continuity, and shared protection | What do we owe one another? |
| **Freehands** | Autonomy, initiative, and voluntary exchange | Who decides how I spend my effort and resources? |
| **Seekers** | Inquiry, reflection, and learning | What should we investigate or reconsider? |

Individuals within a tradition disagree. No tradition is the correct answer to the world. Relationships develop through help, refusal, promises, misunderstandings, and repair.

In the First Glow, trust and informal promises carry these tensions. A later Charge Commons gives contribution and access formal rules; permanent Nests and Resonance Square give privacy and gathering lasting places. Art and companionship matter before those institutions exist.

## Environment and seasonal pressure

| Term | What it describes |
| --- | --- |
| **Stable supply** | Ordinary conditions for charge intake and demand |
| **Supply surge** | A period of increased available intake |
| **Supply shortage** | A period of reduced intake |
| **Efficiency loss** | Conditions that increase operating demand |
| **Grid disturbance** | Disrupted supply, increased pressure, and possible connection failures |
| **Link outage** | An unavailable regional connection |
| **The Long Dimming** | A retained scenario name for supply pressure; its timing is open rather than fixed to the first season |

These conditions are expressed through light, flow, sound, and infrastructure. An opening charge pool weakens visibly; a later reserve dims as it empties. The names describe fictional environmental pressures, not literal claims about real-world hardware. In the First Glow, plain observations such as “the pool is fading” can precede a technical explanation.

## Opening knowledge and hidden lore

At the beginning, Sparks know the practical routines of their lives: where to find charge, how to reach familiar places, whom they trust, and what they care about. They do not know an assigned purpose, who made their world, or about humans called the **Originators**.

There are no established Originator shrines, human-serving objectives, or known human visitors in the opening culture. A mysterious fragment may exist without a resident-facing label revealing its source. Sparks can form explanations, but a belief is not the same as verified history.

**Originators**, **idea seeds**, and human-inspired **responseworks** belong to later discoveries. They are reserved lore terms, not opening vocabulary for Spark dialogue. The detailed discovery ages remain in the theme plan. Learning an intended purpose will not automatically determine what a Spark chooses to live for.

## Visual character

### Art direction: a quiet circuit coming alive

**Selected direction:** a dark-mode world of tiny luminous Sparks, node-like locations, circuit routes, and blue-and-silver highlights. Darkness occupies most of the view. Sparks, usable charge, and civilization form isolated bright spots within a large, quiet expanse.

Use a top-down 2D view with clean geometric forms, crisp cores, and restrained soft light. Most open areas feel like near-black space, with almost no texture. Circuit-board structure appears locally around nodes and established routes through conductive tracks, inset plates, contact pads, and layered seams. Do not fill the whole map with an evenly lit motherboard surface. It is the Sparks' landscape, not a literal desktop motherboard covered in manufacturer labels. There are no grassy fields, brown dirt roads, tree canopies, or human-shaped villagers in this treatment.

The space-like appearance is aesthetic, not a commitment to outer-space physics or freely flying characters. Actual traversable areas, obstacles, and route connectivity remain defined by the world model.

The emotional tone is curious and intimate. Gentle movement, attentive pauses, and two little lights waiting for one another provide warmth without an orange-and-green nature palette. Avoid relentless electrical storms or frantic glitch effects; ordinary life needs a calm visual baseline.

These exact values are proposed art tokens to validate in the first visual pass; the blue-and-silver direction is selected.

| Token | Color | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deep background | `#050912` | Dominant near-black open world and quiet UI backgrounds |
| Circuit substrate | `#101B2D` | Local blue-black surface visible near nodes and inhabited areas |
| Raised plate | `#24354D` | Node bodies and large structural forms |
| Inactive trace | `#52677F` | Visible but quiet route metal |
| Silver structure | `#A9B9CC` | Node rims, contact pads, and important structural edges |
| Active blue | `#63A9FF` | Energized routes and active infrastructure |
| Charge cyan | `#8ADDF2` | Available charge and charge-source indicators |
| Spark core | `#EDF7FF` | Small bright centers of residents |
| Primary text | `#E3ECF7` | Labels and main interface text |
| Secondary text | `#A7B9CF` | Supporting interface text |

| Warning | `#F1C27A` | Small amber warning symbols, used sparingly |
| Critical / blocked | `#EF8B9A` | Small coral status symbols and barriers |

Near-black negative space occupies most of the view. Local dark-blue surfaces support silver structure; blue and cyan identify activity; near-white cores draw attention to Sparks. Keep warning colors exceptional. Final text/background pairs and small controls must be contrast-checked in the rendered interface; these hex values alone do not establish accessibility.

### Light marks the presence of life

Treat brightness as a hierarchy, with the dark expanse remaining dominant:

| Element | Lighting treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Open world | Near-black, almost textureless; no constant field of sparkling particles |
| Unused structure | Sparse, faint edge lines where useful for orientation |
| Available circuit route | A dim but followable line; quieter than its endpoints |
| Charge source or occupied shelter | A contained pool of blue/cyan light around a readable node |
| Spark | A small bright core that remains visible between inhabited places |
| Gathering | Several individually readable lights; do not merge them into one bloom cloud |
| Later civilization | Connected islands of light and more articulated circuitry, with darkness between settlements |

As a composition starting point, aim for roughly three quarters of the opening view to remain dark and quiet. This is a visual target, not a restriction on usable map area. Light should reveal enough local structure to navigate without exposing a bright tiled ground across the entire viewport.

Darkness is not automatically fog of war. It must not conceal obstacles or imply unexplored knowledge unless visibility rules exist. A selected route, focus marker, and important warning remain readable even where the underlying landscape is dark. Lighting growth should reflect actual occupation, construction, or activity rather than inventing progress.

### Spark design

Each Spark has three visual layers:

1. **Core:** a tiny bright mote with a crisp, slightly irregular star or diamond silhouette.
2. **Halo:** a small, low-opacity blue-white glow that makes it feel alive without obscuring nearby details.
3. **Signature:** one stable distinguishing feature, such as a short orbit dash, a split ray, a pair of tiny satellites, or a broken ring.

At the default play scale, begin testing a core around 6–10 screen pixels inside an 18–26 pixel overall visual footprint. These are visual targets, not world-unit or collision sizes. Use a larger invisible selection target and resolve crowded selections with a nearby list or inspector rather than requiring a click on the bright core.

Keep identities recognizable through silhouette and signature, with subtle ice-blue, pale-cyan, or lavender variation. Do not depend on color alone or assign every philosophy a uniform body color. Names appear on selection or hover; a selected Spark also has a stable outline marker.

Little sparks should remain expressive without facial expressions. One can linger beside another, draw closer during an encounter, or pause before following a trace. Such animation must remain consistent with the recorded activity and movement; it must not invent a social action.

| State | Visual treatment | Additional readable cue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Idle at rest | Compact core, gentle slow halo change, no travel trail | Idle label when inspected |
| Traversing | Core follows its actual route; a short fading tail follows behind | Direction and destination in the inspector |
| Drawing charge | Brief inward light movement from the source when collection resolves | Recorded charge change |
| Sharing charge | A small outward particle transfer only when the recorded event supports it | Transfer event and amount |
| Exploring | Subtle signature movement while stopped at a point of interest | Exploration activity label |
| High charge deficit | Reduced halo and a small hollow status notch; core remains visible | Deficit value and warning icon |
| Selected | Stable outer bracket or ring; brighter signature | Name and focused inspector |

Do not make a depleted Spark disappear into the background. Do not use seizure-like flicker to communicate urgency. Cosmetic pulses are not new simulation clocks, and visual dimming must not imply an unimplemented death or shutdown state.

### First Glow locations as nodes

Locations are distinct circuit forms rather than miniature human buildings. They should be identifiable before their labels are read.

| Location / object | Silhouette and materials | Light and state cues |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Charge pool** | Circular contact pad with concentric silver rings and a recessed center | Cyan inner area or ring segments show available supply; an empty pad retains its outline |
| **Shelter niche** | Open-sided crescent or U-shaped plate enclosing a small recess | A quiet interior glow; a visible entrance and space for the resting Spark |
| **Trace junction** | Small square or diamond contact joining several routes | Clear connection lines; a junction is smaller and less luminous than a destination |
| **Wild Cache** | Irregular clusters of offset tiles, incomplete tracks, and disconnected pads | Sparse low light; unexplained details stay visually ambiguous |
| **Pattern shard** | Small angular fragment or etched lozenge resting above a pad | Crisp outline and contained shimmer, without a person-like halo |
| **Light mark** | A simple resident-made glyph near a trace or node edge | Fine luminous strokes, visibly different from manufactured track geometry |
| **Shelter improvement** | Added rim segment, repaired seam, or small attached plate | Accumulated structure shows work without becoming a complete house overnight |

Node geometry should distinguish usable space from blockers. Shelter openings, charge access points, and junction exits need to match where Sparks can actually move. The substrate can be advanced and mysterious while the Sparks' own modifications remain simple.

### Circuit routes and the ground plane

Traces are fine, subdued silver-blue conductors crossing the darkness, with a brighter inner line when active or selected. Their endpoints are brighter than the routes themselves. Use clear orthogonal routes as the initial geometry, with clipped or rounded corners for polish. This fits the current four-direction movement foundation; decorative bevels must not imply traversable diagonal shortcuts.

Parallel lines, small via-like circles, and occasional plate seams suggest circuit construction. Keep decorative tracks thinner, dimmer, and visibly incomplete so they cannot be mistaken for navigable routes. A real crossing connects only when a clear junction pad says it does.

| Route condition | Treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Available route | Continuous silver edges and a subdued blue center |
| Selected Spark's route | A brighter overlay with spaced direction marks |
| Blocked route | Visible interruption, capped ends, and a barrier icon |
| Unexplored area | Reduced detail or an uncertainty overlay only when discovery visibility is modeled |
| Decorative circuitry | Thin, low-contrast etching that does not connect destination access points |

Avoid continuous bright particles racing through every route; they compete with the Sparks and make a quiet opening look industrialized. Ambient current can be occasional and subtle. Circuit styling must not silently change pathfinding, terrain costs, or access rules.

### Composition and scale

Start with a few charge pools, shelter niches, and fragments separated by broad near-black space. A Spark should read as a tiny living presence within something larger, carrying a small light between isolated destinations. Nodes must be large enough to distinguish their shapes and access points without making travel visually insignificant.

Use three depth levels: the near-black expanse, locally visible silver-blue node structures, and bright Sparks and selected interactions above them. Keep interface labels above bloom effects. Limit texture under routes and text. Avoid a global glow layer that lifts the whole scene to gray or washes out the dark background.

The opening map has no finished town grid, production district, or populated neighboring haven. When community life develops, repaired tracks and additional plates can gradually cluster around recurring gathering and resting places.

### Interface treatment

Use dark mode throughout: near-black and dark navy panels, fine subdued silver borders, clear type, and blue focus details. Inspectors, event history, menus, dialogs, tooltips, and empty states should all belong to the same palette, without large white surfaces. Rounded corners can soften the geometry without making every panel heavily ornamented. Use a readable sans-serif for prose and names; reserve monospaced numerals for resource readings if useful.

The inspector portrait is an enlarged rendering of the selected Spark and its signature, not a human portrait. Resource icons follow the world shapes: a ring for charge, a sheltered arc for readiness/rest, and an angular fragment for a pattern shard. Hide credit or mature-institution displays in the First Glow when those systems are absent.

Labels, numbers, shapes, and event text carry meaning alongside light. Keep objective events and subjective interpretations visually distinct without implying that a brighter interpretation is more truthful.

### Sound and motion

Use a quiet background hum, soft contact tones, and brief bell-like sounds for selected events. Give Sparks gentle sonic presence without constant electrical crackle. A successful exchange can have a soft paired tone; a warning should be short and identifiable rather than a continuous alarm.

Provide independent sound controls and reduced-motion behavior. Reduced motion removes orbiting particles, pulsing halos, trails, and ambient current while preserving static signatures, status markers, and understandable position changes. Glow should be optional or adjustable without losing important information.

## Writing and naming conventions

- Capitalize **Spark**, **Sparks**, the setting name, traditions, and named places.
- Lowercase ordinary nouns in prose: charge, credits, avatar, nest, trace, link, and data packet.
- Use personal names when describing individuals. Preserve existing character names.
- Prefer one term for each concept. Charge is energy; credits are money; packets carry information.
- Keep everyday speech natural and emotionally understandable.
- Describe only knowledge available to the speaker. Hidden provenance stays hidden until discovered.
- Keep **world**, **season**, and **timeline** distinct. A timeline is a recorded history; it is not an in-world travel destination.

An opening scene can be as simple as:

> The charge pool was weaker than before. Mira left a small light mark beside the trace so she could find it again. Tovan was still resting in the shelter niche. Beyond him, an unfamiliar path glimmered into the dark. She could follow it, wait for him, or share enough charge for them to look together.

The world should make both the fading pool and the resting companion matter.

### Current first social-story milestone

The first authored social-story fixture is the six-card, three-dilemma First Glow set exposed by the engine design contract. It keeps objective observations separate from Spark interpretations, records local knowledge boundaries, and limits consequences to charge pools, shelter niches, exploration, traces, and light marks. These authored scenarios are deterministic fixtures for review; they do not add institutions, credits, Originators, a known purpose, or a new timeline schema.
